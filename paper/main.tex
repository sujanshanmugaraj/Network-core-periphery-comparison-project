\title{Do Two Notions of ``Core'' Agree?\\
Core--Periphery Structure versus Structural Cohesion, Validated by Network Robustness}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Author(s)}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Affiliation withheld for double-blind review}}

%% ============================================================== ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
The ``core'' of a network is defined in two largely separate ways: by density, as in core--periphery
models and $k$-cores, and by resistance to disconnection, as in Moody and White's structural cohesion.
We compare the two on eight networks from social, biological and infrastructure domains, using network
robustness under targeted node removal as an external yardstick. Node by node, the notions almost
coincide: $k$-coreness and cohesion level have Spearman correlation $\ge0.99$ in every network, and
ordering nodes by cohesion dismantles networks no faster than ordering them by coreness. At the group
level they diverge: in four of the eight networks a single connected $k$-core consists of many
overlapping $k$-components (up to $7.7$ times as many groups), a feature absent from all degree-preserving
randomisations. We introduce \emph{cohesive brokerage}, the number of extra cohesive groups a node
belongs to, and show that removing brokers first beats both coreness and cohesion in all eight networks,
lowering the robustness index by up to $44\%$; the gain grows with the group-level disagreement
(Spearman $0.88$). Rank hybrids of coreness and cohesion do not improve held-out performance. We also
give an exact, certificate-accelerated algorithm for $k$-components and show that a widely used
implementation can return non-maximal components.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
core--periphery structure, k-core, structural cohesion, k-components, node connectivity,
network robustness, targeted attack
\end{IEEEkeywords}

%% ============================================================== INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Almost every network analysis eventually asks which nodes form its ``core''. Two largely separate
literatures answer this question differently. The \emph{core--periphery} tradition, from Borgatti and
Everett's ideal-pattern models~\cite{borgatti2000models} to continuous core scores~\cite{rombach2014core}
and the ubiquitous $k$-core decomposition~\cite{seidman1983network,batagelj2003cores}, defines the core
through \emph{density}: core nodes have many ties, especially to each other. The
\emph{structural cohesion} tradition of White, Harary, Moody and
White~\cite{white2001cohesiveness,moody2003structural} defines it through \emph{resistance to
disconnection}: a group is cohesive to the extent that many nodes must be removed to pull it apart, which
yields a nested hierarchy of $k$-components.

The two notions are related---every $k$-component lies inside the $k$-core---but not equivalent: two
triangles sharing a node form a $2$-core, yet removing the shared node disconnects them. How large is
this difference in real networks, does it matter, and which notion should an analyst use? Despite the
popularity of both ideas, they are rarely compared head-to-head, and we are not aware of a systematic
comparison that also uses an external, functional yardstick.

We compare core--periphery cores ($k$-coreness, discrete Borgatti--Everett, Rombach et al.) with
structural cohesion on eight networks from social, biological and infrastructure domains, and use
network robustness under targeted node removal~\cite{albert2000error,schneider2011mitigation} as the
yardstick. We ask three questions. \textbf{RQ1:} How much do the two notions agree? \textbf{RQ2:} Which one
identifies the nodes whose removal fragments the network fastest? \textbf{RQ3:} Does a hybrid of the two
outperform either alone on networks it was not tuned on? We pre-stated the hypotheses that agreement is
lower in spatial networks (H1), that cohesion-based orderings dismantle clustered networks faster than
coreness (H2), and that a hybrid helps (H3).

Our main finding is that the two notions agree on \emph{who} is central but disagree on \emph{how} the
centre is organised. Node by node, $k$-coreness and cohesion level are almost the same ranking
(Spearman $\ge0.99$ in all eight networks), so H2 and H3 fail: cohesion levels add nothing to coreness
for dismantling. At the level of groups, however, a single connected $k$-core often consists of many
overlapping $k$-components, up to $7.7$ times as many as there are $k$-core groups. This group-level gap is
absent from degree-preserving randomisations and is tied together by a small set of \emph{cohesive
brokers}. Removing brokers first beats $k$-coreness and cohesion in every network, beats degree in four
of eight, and reduces the robustness index by up to $44\%$ relative to coreness, with gains that track the
size of the group-level gap.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A node- and group-level comparison of core--periphery and cohesion cores across three domains,
with simple propositions that characterise how the two can differ (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}) and
measures of group-level disagreement (fragmentation, group Jaccard).
\item Evidence from degree-preserving null models that the group-level gap is a genuine structural
feature, consistent with the classical result that $k$-cores of random graphs are $k$-connected.
\item A functional validation showing that cohesion \emph{levels} do not improve on coreness, whereas a new
parameter-free score, \emph{cohesive brokerage}, identifies critical nodes; an ablation shows that its
signal comes from the higher levels of the cohesion hierarchy, not just articulation points.
\item A held-out evaluation showing that rank hybrids do not help, a negative result that guards against
overfitting claims in this area.
\item An exact, certificate-accelerated $k$-component algorithm with a correctness proof, which also
exposes non-maximal output of the widely used NetworkX implementation; all code is released.
\end{enumerate}

%% ============================================================== RELATED WORK
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Core--periphery structure.} Borgatti and Everett~\cite{borgatti2000models} formalised the
core--periphery (CP) ideal---a densely connected core and a periphery tied to the core but not to itself---and
fitted it by maximising the correlation between the adjacency matrix and an ideal pattern, in discrete
and continuous variants. Holme~\cite{holme2005core} proposed a closeness-based CP coefficient measured
against degree-preserving randomisations. Rombach et al.~\cite{rombach2014core,rombach2017revisited}
replaced the binary partition by a family of transition profiles and aggregated over them to obtain a
continuous core score. Later work detects multiple CP pairs~\cite{kojaku2017finding} or fits generative
block models~\cite{zhang2015identification}; Gallagher et al.~\cite{gallagher2021clarified} showed that
``hub-and-spoke'' and ``layered'' CP structures are distinct and that methods disagree on which they
find. Csermely et al.~\cite{csermely2013structure} review the functional roles attributed to cores. A
common thread is that these notions define the core by \emph{density}.

\textbf{k-cores.} Seidman~\cite{seidman1983network} introduced $k$-cores as a minimum-degree relaxation
of cohesive subgroups; minimum degree $k$ is necessary but not sufficient for $k$-connectivity. Core decomposition takes linear time~\cite{batagelj2003cores}, and coreness is
a popular centrality: Kitsak et al.~\cite{kitsak2010identification} found it predicts spreading influence
better than degree, while Liu et al.~\cite{liu2015core} showed that densely knit ``core-like groups''
weakly attached to the rest of the network inflate coreness and mislead such rankings---a phenomenon
closely related to the gap studied here. Burleson-Lesser et al.~\cite{burleson2020kcore} linked $k$-core
structure to the robustness of ecological and financial systems.

\textbf{Structural cohesion.} White and Harary~\cite{white2001cohesiveness} argued that cohesion should
be measured by node connectivity rather than density, and Moody and White~\cite{moody2003structural}
defined the nested hierarchy of $k$-components and embeddedness. Exact computation is costly; Torrents
and Ferraro~\cite{torrents2015structural} proposed heuristics that underlie the NetworkX
implementation~\cite{hagberg2008exploring}. Connectivity testing relies on classical results of
Even~\cite{even1975algorithm} and Esfahanian~\cite{esfahanian2013connectivity}, and for random graphs
{\L}uczak~\cite{luczak1991size} showed that $k$-cores are $k$-connected with high probability.

\textbf{Robustness and dismantling.} Albert et al.~\cite{albert2000error} contrasted random failures
with degree-targeted attacks; Schneider et al.~\cite{schneider2011mitigation} introduced the robustness
index $R$; and optimal-percolation methods~\cite{morone2015influence} provide state-of-the-art adaptive
dismantling. Robustness gives an external, functional yardstick for comparing notions of ``core'',
which we exploit.

\textbf{Gap.} CP models and $k$-cores on the one hand, and structural cohesion on the other, are rarely
compared directly. We are not aware of a study that (i) quantifies their agreement at both the node and
the group level across domains, (ii) tests the gap against a degree-preserving null model and (iii)
asks which notion better identifies structurally critical nodes.

%% ============================================================== PRELIMINARIES
\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}
Let $G=(V,E)$ be a simple, undirected, connected graph with $N=|V|$ nodes and $M=|E|$ edges,
$N(v)$ the neighbourhood and $d_v=|N(v)|$ the degree of $v$, and $G[S]$ the subgraph induced by $S\subseteq V$.

\begin{definition}[k-core, coreness~\cite{seidman1983network}]
The $k$-core of $G$ is the maximal induced subgraph with minimum degree at least $k$; it is obtained by
repeatedly deleting nodes of degree $<k$. The \emph{coreness} $\cn_v$ is the largest $k$ such that $v$
belongs to the $k$-core. The \emph{degeneracy} is $k_{\max}=\max_v \cn_v$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Structural cohesion~\cite{moody2003structural,white2001cohesiveness}]
The node connectivity $\kappa(H)$ of a graph $H$ is the minimum number of nodes whose removal
disconnects $H$ (for a complete graph, $|H|-1$). By Menger's theorem~\cite{menger1927allgemeinen},
$\kappa(H)\ge k$ iff every pair of nodes is joined by $k$ internally node-disjoint paths. A
\emph{$k$-component} is a maximal set $K\subseteq V$ with $|K|>k$ and $\kappa(G[K])\ge k$. The
\emph{cohesion level} $\coh_v$ is the largest $k$ such that $v$ lies in some $k$-component.
\end{definition}

$k$-components nest: every $(k{+}1)$-component lies inside a $k$-component, which yields Moody and
White's hierarchy of embeddedness~\cite{moody2003structural}. Unlike connected components, distinct
$k$-components may overlap. We exploit that overlap explicitly:

\begin{definition}[Cohesive brokerage]
Let $m_k(v)$ be the number of $k$-components that contain $v$. The \emph{cohesive brokerage} of $v$ is
$b_v=\sum_{k\ge 2}\max\{0,\,m_k(v)-1\}$, i.e.\ the number of \emph{additional} cohesive groups a node
belongs to, summed over all levels of the hierarchy. Nodes with $b_v>0$ are \emph{brokers}.
\end{definition}

At $k=2$, the $2$-components are the biconnected components with at least three nodes and the brokers
are the articulation points shared by them; for $k\ge3$ brokerage generalises articulation to small
vertex sets that glue together groups that cannot be merged into one $k$-cohesive group.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:le}
$\coh_v\le \cn_v$ for every node $v$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $K$ is a $k$-component, then $G[K]$ is $k$-connected with $|K|>k$, so every node of $G[K]$ has at
least $k$ neighbours in $K$ (otherwise its neighbourhood, of size $<k$, would separate it). Hence $K$
belongs to the $k$-core and $\cn_v\ge k$ for all $v\in K$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:groups}
Every $k$-component lies inside a single connected component of the $k$-core. A connected component
of the $k$-core that is itself $k$-connected is a $k$-component.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first claim follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:le} and the connectivity of $G[K]$. For the second,
let $C$ be such a component and $K\supseteq C$ a $k$-component; by the first claim $K$ lies in the
component $C$ of the $k$-core, so $K=C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:overlap}
Two distinct $k$-components share at most $k-1$ nodes.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $K_1\ne K_2$ share $\ge k$ nodes and let $S$ be any set of $<k$ nodes. Both $G[K_1]-S$
and $G[K_2]-S$ are connected and they share a node outside $S$, so $G[K_1\cup K_2]-S$ is connected.
Hence $K_1\cup K_2$ is $k$-connected, contradicting the maximality of $K_1$ and $K_2$.
\end{proof}

Propositions~\ref{prop:le}--\ref{prop:groups} make precise \emph{how} the two notions of ``core'' can
differ: a node may sit deeper in the $k$-core hierarchy than in the cohesion hierarchy (a node-level
gap), and, even where the node levels coincide, one connected $k$-core can split into several
overlapping $k$-components (a group-level gap). Proposition~\ref{prop:overlap} shows that such splits
are always glued together by fewer than $k$ shared nodes, the brokers.

\textbf{Example (bowtie).} Take two triangles $\{A,B,X\}$ and $\{X,C,D\}$ sharing node $X$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bowtie}a). Every node has degree $\ge2$, so the $2$-core is the whole graph and
$\cn_v=2$ for all $v$. Removing $X$ disconnects the graph, so $\kappa(G)=1$, while each triangle is a
$2$-component. Thus $\coh_v=\cn_v=2$ for every node, but the single $2$-core group consists of two
$2$-components overlapping in the broker $X$ ($b_X=1$). The node-level measures agree perfectly; the
group-level structure does not, and the disagreement singles out the most critical node.

\textbf{Random graphs.} For Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs the $k$-core, when it exists, is
$k$-connected with high probability~\cite{luczak1991size}. Hence, in a random graph, both node-level
and group-level gaps vanish asymptotically, which makes degree-preserving randomisation a natural null
model for both gaps (Section~\ref{sec:null}).

%% ============================================================== METHODS
\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Core--periphery scores}
\textbf{k-core coreness.} We use the $O(M)$ peeling algorithm of Batagelj and
Zaver\v{s}nik~\cite{batagelj2003cores}.

\textbf{Discrete Borgatti--Everett (BE).} Each node is labelled core ($x_v=1$) or periphery
($x_v=0$). The ideal pattern is $\delta_{uv}=1$ unless both $u$ and $v$ are periphery, and the fit is the
Pearson correlation $\rho$ between $\{A_{uv}\}_{u<v}$ and $\{\delta_{uv}\}_{u<v}$~\cite{borgatti2000models}.
With $\Pi=N(N-1)/2$ node pairs, $n_p$ periphery nodes, $E_{pp}$ periphery--periphery edges,
$P=\Pi-n_p(n_p-1)/2$ and $\Sigma=M-E_{pp}$,
\begin{equation}
\rho=\frac{\Pi\,\Sigma-M P}{\sqrt{(\Pi M-M^2)(\Pi P-P^2)}}.
\label{eq:be}
\end{equation}
Flipping one node changes $n_p$ by one and $E_{pp}$ by its number of periphery neighbours, so the effect
of a flip is evaluated in $O(1)$ and applied in $O(d_v)$. We run greedy label switching: starting from the
top $10\%$ of nodes by degree as core, visit nodes in random order, flip whenever $\rho$ increases and
stop after a pass without improvement; we keep the best of $20$ random visiting orders.

\textbf{Rombach et al.} Nodes are assigned the values of the profile~\cite[Eq.~9]{rombach2014core}
\begin{equation}
C^*_i=\begin{cases}\dfrac{i(1-\alpha)}{2\lfloor\beta N\rfloor}, & i\le\lfloor\beta N\rfloor,\\[2mm]
\dfrac{(i-\lfloor\beta N\rfloor)(1-\alpha)}{2(N-\lfloor\beta N\rfloor)}+\dfrac{1+\alpha}{2}, & i>\lfloor\beta N\rfloor,\end{cases}
\end{equation}
normalised to unit sum, and the assignment (a permutation) maximising the core quality
$R_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{u,v}A_{uv}C_uC_v$ is sought. The aggregate core score is
\begin{equation}
CS(v)=Z\sum_{\alpha,\beta}C_v(\alpha,\beta)\,R_{\alpha,\beta},
\end{equation}
with $Z$ such that $\max_v CS(v)=1$. We use
$\alpha\in\{0,0.2,\dots,1\}$ and $\beta\in\{0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9\}$ ($54$ pairs; the original paper uses a
$100\times100$ grid, which is computationally prohibitive for our larger networks). For each pair we run
simulated annealing~\cite{kirkpatrick1983optimization} over swaps of two nodes' values, started from the
degree ordering, with $\max(500N,3{\times}10^5)$ proposals and geometric cooling from $T_0$ (the mean
$|\Delta R|$ of 2000 random proposals) to $10^{-6}T_0$. The change in $R$ of a swap is
$\Delta R=2\Delta_u(s_u-s_v)-2A_{uv}\Delta_u^2$ with $s=AC$ and $\Delta_u=C_v-C_u$, so each proposal costs
$O(\log d_u)$ and each accepted move $O(d_u+d_v)$.

\subsection{Exact structural cohesion}
\label{sec:cohesion-alg}
NetworkX's \texttt{k\_components}~\cite{hagberg2008exploring}, which follows the heuristics of Torrents and
Ferraro~\cite{torrents2015structural}, is the standard tool for this task. We found that it can return
\emph{non-maximal} components (e.g.\ for $k=3$ in the Les Mis\'erables graph it reports a $32$-node set that
is strictly contained in a $3$-connected $34$-node set; see also Table~\ref{tab:bench}). We therefore
implement an exact algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg:kcomp}) and verify it against exhaustive enumeration.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Exact $k$-components by certified cut splitting}
\label{alg:kcomp}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\mathcal{K}_2\gets$ biconnected components of $G$ with $\ge3$ nodes~\cite{hopcroft1973algorithm}
\State stack $\gets\{(K,3):K\in\mathcal{K}_2\}$
\While{stack not empty}
  \State $(P,k)\gets$ pop; $H\gets$ $k$-core of $G[P]$
  \For{each connected component $C$ of $H$ with $|C|>k$}
    \State $S\gets$ \Call{SmallCut}{$G[C],k$}
    \If{$S=\emptyset$} \State add $C$ to $\mathcal{K}_k$; push $(C,k{+}1)$
    \Else \State push $(D\cup S,k)$ for each component $D$ of $G[C]-S$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndWhile
\Function{SmallCut}{$H,k$} \Comment{a cut of size $<k$, or $\emptyset$}
  \State $v\gets$ a minimum-degree node of $H$
  \For{each pair $(x,y)\in\mathcal{P}_v$} \Comment{see text}
    \If{\textbf{not} \Call{Cert}{$x,y,k$} \textbf{and} $\kappa(x,y)<k$}
      \State \Return a minimum $x$--$y$ vertex cut
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\emptyset$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Correctness.} \emph{(i) Splitting is safe.} If $S$ is a vertex cut of $G[C]$ with $|S|<k$ and $K$
is a $k$-component inside $C$, then $G[K]-S$ is connected, so $K\setminus S$ lies within one component
$D$ of $G[C]-S$ and $K\subseteq D\cup S$. Pruning to the $k$-core never removes a node of $K$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:le}). Hence no $k$-component is lost, and a piece that is $k$-connected equals the
$k$-component containing it, i.e.\ it is maximal. The recursion terminates because each piece $D\cup S$
is a proper subset of $C$. \emph{(ii) The test is complete.} With $v$ of minimum degree, $G[C]$ is
$k$-connected iff $\kappa(x,y)\ge k$ for every pair in
$\mathcal{P}_v=\{(v,w):w\notin N(v)\cup\{v\}\}\cup\{(x,y):x,y\in N(v),\,xy\notin E\}$, i.e.\ for $v$ and each
of its non-neighbours and for each non-adjacent pair of its
neighbours~\cite{even1975algorithm,esfahanian2013connectivity}.

\textbf{Certificates.} Most pairs ($92\%$ in our networks, Section~\ref{sec:results}) are certified
without a max-flow computation:
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cert}
Let $T_x$ be the smallest set that contains $x$ and $N(x)$, contains every node verified by max-flow
to satisfy $\kappa(x,\cdot)\ge k$, and contains every node with at least $k$ neighbours in $T_x$
(``bootstrap closure''). Then $\kappa(x,y)\ge k$ for every $y\in T_x$. Moreover, $\kappa(x,y)\ge k$
whenever $|N(x)\cap N(y)|\ge k$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The second claim holds because common neighbours give $k$ internally disjoint paths of length two. For
the first, let $S$ be any vertex set with $|S|<k$ and $x\notin S$. We show by induction over the order
of insertion that every $w\in T_x\setminus S$ is connected to $x$ in $G-S$. This holds for $x$, for
neighbours of $x$ and for flow-verified nodes. A node added by the closure has at least $k>|S|$
neighbours in $T_x$, so at least one of them, inserted earlier, lies outside $S$ and is connected to $x$
by the induction hypothesis.
\end{proof}
Remaining pairs are decided by a unit-capacity augmenting-path max-flow on the node-split graph with
cutoff $k$, compiled with Numba~\cite{lam2015numba}; a minimum vertex cut is read off the residual graph.

\subsection{Agreement measures (RQ1)}
\textbf{Node level.} For each pair of score vectors we report Spearman's $\rho_s$; the Jaccard overlap
of the top-$x\%$ sets ($x\in\{5,10,20\}$), where a top set contains every node scoring at least the
$\lceil xN\rceil$-th largest value (so tied nodes are included together and identical rankings give
overlap $1$; the lowest tier, e.g.\ the BE periphery, is never part of a top set); and the adjusted mutual information
(AMI)~\cite{vinh2010information} between binary core labels (main $k$-core; deepest $k$-components; the BE
labelling; the top-$|\text{BE core}|$ nodes by $CS$).
\textbf{Group level.} For every $k\ge2$ we compare the connected components of the $k$-core
($n_{cc}(k)$ groups) with the $k$-components ($n_{kc}(k)$ groups) through the \emph{fragmentation}
$F=\sum_k n_{kc}(k)/\sum_k n_{cc}(k)$ (by Proposition~\ref{prop:groups}, $F=1$ when every $k$-core group
is $k$-cohesive), the \emph{coverage} (fraction of $k$-core nodes that lie in some $k$-component) and the
\emph{group Jaccard} $J_G$: the size-weighted best-match Jaccard similarity of each $k$-core group to a
$k$-component. Coverage and $J_G$ are averaged over $k$ with weights equal to the $k$-core size.

\subsection{Null model}
\label{sec:null}
For each network we generate $100$ degree-preserving randomisations by $10M$ accepted double-edge
swaps~\cite{maslov2002specificity} (rejecting self-loops and multi-edges), keep the largest connected
component, and recompute all statistics (BE uses $5$ restarts). We report $z$-scores and empirical
two-sided $p$-values $p=\min\{1,2\min(p_{\ge},p_{\le})\}$ with $p_{\ge}=(1+\#\{\text{null}\ge\text{obs}\})/101$.

\subsection{Robustness experiment (RQ2)}
Nodes are removed in a given order; $S(q)$ is the size of the largest connected component, relative to
$N$, after removing a fraction $q$ of nodes, and $R=\frac1N\sum_{Q=1}^{N}S(Q/N)$~\cite{schneider2011mitigation}.
A lower $R$ means the ordering found more critical nodes. Curves are computed exactly in
$O(M\,\alpha(N))$ time by re-inserting nodes in reverse order with union--find~\cite{newman2000efficient}.
We compare static orderings by degree, betweenness (exact, Brandes~\cite{brandes2001faster}), $k$-core
coreness, BE labels, Rombach $CS$, cohesion level and cohesive brokerage, against the mean of $30$ random
orderings and an \emph{adaptive} degree attack (recomputing degrees after every removal)~\cite{albert2000error},
a strong and widely used baseline. Coreness, cohesion and brokerage take few distinct values, so ties
matter: our main analysis breaks ties by degree (then node index); as a sensitivity analysis we break
all ties uniformly at random and average $R$ over $50$ draws.

\subsection{Hybrid scores (RQ3)}
With $r(\cdot)$ the rank of a score normalised to $[0,1]$, we define
$H_v(\lambda)=\lambda\,r(\cn_v)+(1-\lambda)\,r(x_v)$, with $x=\coh$ (\emph{core+cohesion}, as originally
planned) or $x=b$ (\emph{core+brokerage}), ties broken by degree. The weight
$\lambda\in\{0,0.1,\dots,1\}$ is chosen on training networks by minimising the mean of $R(\lambda)/R_{\text{k-core}}$
(ties resolved towards larger $\lambda$) and evaluated only on held-out networks, using (i) a
domain-stratified 2-fold split (fold A: Karate, Email-EU, Yeast, Power grid; fold B: GrQc, Facebook,
C.~elegans, Air India), applied in both directions, and (ii) leave-one-network-out.

\subsection{Statistical comparison}
With eight networks we report, for each pair of orderings, the mean difference in $R$ with a $95\%$
bootstrap confidence interval ($10^4$ resamples of networks~\cite{efron1979bootstrap}), win counts, the
exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} and the matched-pairs
rank-biserial correlation $r_{rb}$ as effect size~\cite{kerby2014simple}; $p$-values are Holm-adjusted
within each family of comparisons~\cite{holm1979simple}. With $n=8$ the smallest attainable exact
two-sided $p$ is $2/2^8\approx0.008$, so we emphasise effect sizes over significance.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}
We study eight networks from three domains (Table~\ref{tab:data}). \emph{Social:} Zachary's karate
club~\cite{zachary1977information}; the arXiv General Relativity co-authorship network
(GrQc)~\cite{leskovec2007graph}; the e-mail network of a European research institution
(Email-EU)~\cite{yin2017local,leskovec2007graph}; and the combined Facebook ego
networks~\cite{mcauley2012learning}, all from SNAP~\cite{snapnets}. \emph{Biological:} the yeast
protein--protein interaction network of Collins et al.~\cite{collins2007toward} and the
\emph{C.~elegans} neural network~\cite{white1986structure,watts1998collective}, both from

\section{Results}

\subsection{RQ1: Do the two notions of core agree?}
\textbf{Node level: almost perfectly for k-cores, weakly for CP models.} Table~\ref{tab:agree} and
Figs.~\ref{fig:heat}--\ref{fig:nodegroup} summarise agreement with the cohesion level $\coh$. $k$-core
coreness and cohesion are nearly interchangeable as rankings: Spearman $\rho_s\ge0.99$ and top-$10\%$
Jaccard $J_{10}\ge0.96$ in every network, and the main core coincides exactly with the deepest
$k$-components (AMI $=1$) in all eight. The fraction of \emph{gap nodes} ($\cn_v>\coh_v$) is zero in four
networks and at most $15.7\%$ (Facebook); because $87\%$ of gap nodes lose a single level, the
cohesion deficit $1-\sum_v\coh_v/\sum_v\cn_v$ never exceeds $1\%$. The classical CP models agree
much less: Rombach's $CS$ reaches $\rho_s=0.73$--$0.99$ and BE's binary labels only $\rho_s=0.24$--$0.54$.
Across domains, agreement of the CP models with cohesion is lowest for the infrastructure networks
(mean $\rho_s$ for $CS$: social $0.92$, biological $0.95$, infrastructure $0.85$), which is the pattern
anticipated by H1 for spatial networks; for $k$-coreness, however, agreement is uniformly near perfect
($0.994$--$0.999$), so H1 is \emph{not} supported for the most widely used CP proxy.

\textbf{Group level: substantial disagreement in half of the networks.} Node levels hide how the cores
are organised. In Email-EU and \emph{C.~elegans} every connected $k$-core group is $k$-cohesive at every
$k$ ($F=1$, $J_G=1$): the two notions of core are identical. In GrQc, Facebook, Yeast and the power grid,
by contrast, a single connected $k$-core repeatedly breaks into many overlapping $k$-components
($F=4.2$--$7.7$; Fig.~\ref{fig:groupk}). GrQc's $2$-core is one connected group but contains $260$
$2$-components; on Facebook, $k$-core groups overlap their best-matching $k$-component with a
size-weighted Jaccard of only $J_G=0.53$.
The cores are therefore \emph{the same nodes, organised differently}: coverage stays $\ge0.98$, so almost
every $k$-core node does belong to some $k$-component, but the $k$-core glues several cohesive groups
together through a small number of brokers ($71$--$558$ nodes, $1$--$13\%$ of the network).

\textbf{What drives the gap?} Across the eight networks, the size-normalised group disagreement
$1-J_G$ increases with transitivity ($\rho_s=0.67$) and degree assortativity ($0.64$), but not with
size ($0.40$), and the share of gap nodes is strongly associated with assortativity ($\rho_s=0.88$,
$p=0.004$). The raw fragmentation $F$, in contrast, grows with network size ($\rho_s=0.78$), because
larger networks simply contain more groups; we therefore treat $F$ as descriptive and rely on the
null model to separate structure from size.

\textbf{The gap is not an artefact of the degree sequence.} In degree-preserving randomisations of the
same networks the gap vanishes (Table~\ref{tab:null}, Fig.~\ref{fig:null}): randomised graphs have
essentially no gap nodes, $F\approx1$ and no brokers, as predicted by {\L}uczak's theorem for random
graphs~\cite{luczak1991size}. For GrQc, Facebook, Yeast and the power grid the observed fragmentation and
broker fraction lie above all $100$ randomisations (empirical $p=0.02$, the smallest attainable value).
Only in the two smallest networks (Karate, Air India) do randomisations occasionally produce brokers,
a finite-size effect. Interestingly, the BE correlation of five of the eight empirical networks is
significantly \emph{lower} than that of their randomisations (e.g.\ $z=-83$ for Facebook, $-54$ for GrQc
and $-48$ for Yeast; $p=0.02$ in all five): clustered networks are less
core--periphery-like, in the BE sense, than random graphs with the same degrees---plausibly because
rewiring replaces local, clustered ties by ties among high-degree nodes, which the BE pattern rewards. Of the two notions, it is the cohesion structure---not the density-based
CP fit---that carries non-random signal in these networks.

\subsection{RQ2: Which notion finds the critical nodes?}
Table~\ref{tab:rob} lists the robustness index of every ordering, Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} shows the
curves, and Table~\ref{tab:cmp} the paired comparisons.

\textbf{Cohesion level does not beat coreness.} Because the two rankings are almost identical, so are
their attacks: the mean difference is $\overline{\Delta R}=+0.002$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.001,0.005]$; $2$ wins,
$3$ losses, $3$ exact ties). H2 is therefore not supported. Both are clearly \emph{worse} than plain
degree ($\overline{\Delta R}=+0.029$ for coreness, $0/8$ wins, $r_{rb}=1$), confirming that deep
$k$-shells are not where a network is most vulnerable~\cite{liu2015core}. Rombach's $CS$ is worse still
on average, while BE's ``core first, then by degree'' ordering is statistically indistinguishable from degree.

\textbf{Cohesive brokerage does.} Promoting brokers to the front of the degree ordering beats both
$k$-coreness and cohesion in all eight networks ($\overline{\Delta R}=-0.049$, CI $[-0.086,-0.017]$,
$r_{rb}=-1$), and beats or ties degree in six of eight ($\overline{\Delta R}=-0.020$, CI $[-0.047,0.000]$).
The gains are concentrated where the group-level gap is large: relative to degree, brokerage lowers $R$
by $24\%$ on Facebook and $33\%$ on Yeast, and relative to $k$-coreness by $33$--$44\%$ on Facebook,
GrQc and Yeast; on Email-EU and \emph{C.~elegans}, which have no brokers, it reduces to degree. Across
networks, the advantage of brokerage over $k$-coreness tracks the group disagreement $1-J_G$
($\rho_s=0.88$, $p=0.004$), and so does its advantage over degree ($\rho_s=0.75$, $p=0.03$), whereas the
latter is unrelated to size ($\rho_s=0.08$). Brokerage is not a universal winner: betweenness, which
measures brokerage along shortest paths, remains the strongest static ordering on GrQc, Email-EU,
Facebook and Yeast, and an adaptive degree attack is stronger than brokerage in six of eight networks.
Among static orderings, brokerage's mean rank ($2.44$) is comparable to BE ($2.25$), betweenness
($2.75$) and degree ($2.81$), and far ahead of $k$-coreness ($5.56$) and cohesion ($5.81$); unlike
betweenness it requires no shortest-path computation once the cohesion hierarchy is known. With eight networks and Holm correction over twelve comparisons, no adjusted $p$-value falls
below $0.094$ (the minimum attainable is $\approx0.09$); the conclusions rest on the consistent direction
and the effect sizes.

\textbf{Sensitivity to tie-breaking.} When ties are broken uniformly at random
(Table~\ref{tab:cmp}, right; Fig.~\ref{fig:rel}b), every coarse score deteriorates, and brokerage most
of all, since $86$--$100\%$ of nodes share the value $b=0$. Brokerage is thus a sparse
\emph{prioritisation layer}: it tells which nodes to remove first, and must be combined with a base
ranking for the rest. Coreness and cohesion likewise depend on their tie-breaker: their advantage over
random removal shrinks in every network (e.g.\ in the power grid from $0.129$ to $0.055$ for
coreness), which underlines that tie-breaking rules must be reported in robustness studies.

\subsection{RQ3: Does a hybrid help?}
Neither hybrid improves on its better component on held-out networks (Table~\ref{tab:hyb},
Fig.~\ref{fig:hyb}). For core$+$cohesion the two inputs are nearly the same ranking; intermediate
weights mix two degree tie-breaks and are slightly worse ($\approx1\%$) than either extreme, the selected
$\lambda^*$ flips between $0$ and $1$ across folds, and the held-out hybrid never wins (H3 not supported).
For core$+$brokerage the training folds always select $\lambda^*=0$, i.e.\ pure brokerage: adding
coreness to brokerage only hurts (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyb}b), so the best ``hybrid'' is brokerage itself,
which matches the conclusion of RQ2.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Two notions, one ranking, two organisations.} Proposition~\ref{prop:le} guarantees
$\coh_v\le\cn_v$, and our data show that the inequality is almost always tight: real $k$-cores are
almost $k$-cohesive \emph{node by node}. The density view and the connectivity view therefore rank nodes
nearly identically, and for tasks that only need a ranking---for example choosing seeds or computing a
centrality---the linear-time $k$-core is an excellent proxy for the far more expensive cohesion
level. The two views part ways at the level of \emph{groups}. In highly clustered networks (GrQc,
Facebook, Yeast) and in the sparse power grid, $k$-cores are federations of cohesive groups joined
through a few shared nodes; in Email-EU and \emph{C.~elegans}, which are dense and well mixed, they are
not. This matches the intuition behind ``core-like groups''~\cite{liu2015core} and structural
holes~\cite{burt1992structural}: density aggregates groups that connectivity keeps apart. It also explains why
the CP models agree less with cohesion: they are tuned to a single dense core, while the cohesion
hierarchy is inherently plural.

\textbf{Implications.} Analysts who use $k$-cores to describe ``the core'' of a network should check whether
it is cohesive; a $k$-core with $F\gg1$ is better described as a set of overlapping cohesive groups. For
robustness and vulnerability analyses, the useful output of the cohesion hierarchy is not a node's level
but its \emph{multiplicity}: brokers that sit in several $k$-components at once are where the hierarchy
can be cut. Cohesive brokerage is parameter-free, reuses the decomposition, and gave its largest gains on
the networks where the group-level gap is large---a property that can be measured before any attack is
simulated.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) Eight networks support statements about our sample, not about networks in
general; all comparisons are reported with effect sizes, and none survives multiple-testing correction at
$\alpha=0.05$ given the minimum attainable $p$. (ii) All networks were reduced to simple, undirected,
unweighted largest components; weighted or directed cohesion may behave differently. (iii) We study
static orderings and the robustness index $R$; adaptive or optimal-percolation
attacks~\cite{morone2015influence} are stronger, and other functional criteria (spreading, synchronisation)
may rank the notions differently. Adaptive degree, our strongest baseline, beats brokerage in six
networks, and an adaptive variant of brokerage (recomputing the hierarchy after removals) is a natural
next step. (iv) For tractability, Rombach's method used a $6\times9$ grid instead of $100\times100$, and BE
was optimised greedily from 20 random visiting orders, so both fits may be slightly sub-optimal and the
corresponding results are conservative for these methods. (v) Our null model preserves degrees but not clustering; a clustering-preserving null
model would test whether the group-level gap exceeds what triangles alone imply. (vi) Exact cohesion
remains costly: networks of a few thousand nodes take up to a minute and a half, but very large
networks would require approximation or parallelisation.

\section{Conclusion}
Core--periphery cores and structural-cohesion cores agree on who is central but not on how the centre is
organised. Across eight networks, $k$-coreness and cohesion level are practically the same ranking, so
cohesion levels neither change the identified core nor improve targeted attacks, and hybrids of the two
do not help. The disagreement lives at the group level: in half of the networks one connected $k$-core
is a federation of several overlapping cohesive groups, a feature that is absent from degree-preserving
randomisations. The nodes that hold these federations together, the cohesive brokers, are critical:
removing them first dismantles the networks markedly faster than coreness, and the gain grows with the size
of the group-level gap. We hope that these results, the exact algorithm and the released code help
analysts decide when a dense core is also a cohesive one.
